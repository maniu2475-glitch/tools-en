Why time zone conversion is harder than it looks

A time zone is not a fixed offset from UTC. It is a set of rules — a base offset plus a list of historical and future transitions. Most of North America and Europe shift one hour twice a year for daylight saving time, the Southern Hemisphere shifts on opposite dates, and a handful of places shift by 30 or 45 minutes instead of a full hour, such as India at UTC+05:30 and Nepal at UTC+05:45. Australia even has zones that observe a half-hour daylight offset. Any converter that simply adds or subtracts whole hours will therefore produce wrong answers for a large part of the world.

How this converter gets it right

This page does not hard-code offsets. It asks the browser for the wall-clock time in each zone using Intl.DateTimeFormat with an explicit timeZone, then derives the real offset from that result. The browser ships with the IANA time zone database (also called tzdata or the Olson database), which is updated whenever governments change their rules — Brazil abolishing daylight saving, Mexico ending it in most states, Egypt reintroducing it. Because the conversion runs locally, the tool works offline after the page loads and no meeting time is ever sent to a server.

Practical tips for scheduling across zones

Always convert the instant, not the clock reading. When a colleague says "let's meet at 9am my time", the safe record is the UTC instant plus the zone identifier, never a bare "9am". Store ISO-8601 strings with an explicit offset such as 2026-11-19T09:00:00-05:00 and keep the IANA name alongside, because offsets change with policy. Watch for the day-boundary problem shown in the table's "day diff" column: a 9am call in New York is already tomorrow in Tokyo. If you also need to turn timestamps into human dates, pair this tool with the Unix timestamp converter or the date difference calculator.
