ROI Calculator

Return on Investment (ROI) measures how much profit an investment produced relative to what it cost. What is ROI and why does it matter?

Return on Investment, usually shortened to ROI, is the single most used profitability ratio in business and personal finance. It answers one simple question: for every dollar I put in, how many dollars did I get back beyond my original stake? Because it is expressed as a percentage rather than an absolute number, ROI lets you compare a small short-term trade, a rental property held for a decade, and an advertising campaign on the same scale. If a project returns 40% and another returns 12%, the first one converted capital into profit more efficiently, regardless of which one involved more money.

How this calculator works

The calculator first adds any extra costs, fees, commissions or taxes you enter to the initial investment to get a total cost. It then subtracts that total cost from the final value to produce the net profit. Dividing net profit by total cost and multiplying by 100 gives the ROI percentage. A positive ROI means you recovered more than you spent; a negative ROI means you took a loss, and the calculator will show that clearly rather than hiding it.

If you supply a holding period in months, the tool also computes the annualized ROI. A 35% gain over two years is not the same achievement as a 35% gain over three months, yet plain ROI shows both identically. Annualizing converts the result into a per-year equivalent, which is the only honest way to compare investments of different durations. The calculator also reports your multiple of money (final value divided by total cost), so you can see at a glance whether an investment tripled, doubled, or merely kept pace with inflation.

Worked example

Suppose you invest $10,000 in a small index-fund position, pay $0 in extra fees, and sell after 24 months for $13,500. Net profit is $3,500, so ROI is 3500 / 10000 = 35%. Annualized ROI is (1 + 0.35)^(1/2) − 1 = 16.2% per year, which is a far more meaningful figure if you want to know whether that beat a savings account. If instead you had held for only six months and earned the same 35%, the annualized result would be roughly 88% — a very different story.

Common mistakes to avoid

The most frequent error is forgetting hidden costs. Trading commissions, platform fees, property taxes, legal costs and capital-gains taxes all reduce real ROI. Enter them in the additional costs field. The second error is ignoring time: a 20% return after ten years is worse than a 12% return after one year, because the long investment tied up your capital for a decade. Finally, remember that ROI is a nominal number — it does not automatically account for inflation or risk. A 6% ROI in a year with 5% inflation is nearly flat in purchasing-power terms, and a 40% ROI on a speculative bet is not comparable to a 40% ROI on a government bond. Use ROI as a starting point for comparison, not as the only metric.
